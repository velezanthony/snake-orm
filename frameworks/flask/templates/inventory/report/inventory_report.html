Four questions a plain filter() cannot answer, and the ratio they add up to. Five statements in total, and not one of them grows with the number of stock rows — every figure below arrived as an aggregate, computed by the engine and never by this page.

What each warehouse holds

annotate() — two correlated aggregates, COUNT and SUM, riding in the same statement as the warehouse row. A warehouse with nothing in it still appears, with zeroes: that is the row a GROUP BY over the stock would have dropped, and it is the one an "is this site actually being used" question is about.

The SKUs that actually move

group_by() + having() — the rows were folded on the SKU's name and then filtered by the aggregate itself, which is the one thing a WHERE cannot do. Threshold applied: at least {{ minimum_moves }} movement(s). It is printed because a filtered list whose filter is not named is a list nobody can reproduce.

A negative net means the SKU has shipped more than it received. That is the interesting row, not an error: the figure is signed and stays an integer because it counts units, not money.

Where each row ranks inside its own warehouse

A window function — rank() partitioned by warehouse. This is the column no filter and no GROUP BY could produce: it is a fact about the row's neighbours, computed without collapsing them. Ties share a position, because two SKUs holding the same number of units really are tied.

Total so far, and total lately

sum(delta).over(order_by=...) is the RUNNING total — the default frame reaches from the start of the series to this row, so it only ever grows.

frame=snake_rows(snake_preceding(2), SNAKE_CURRENT_ROW) is the MOVING one: a window three movements wide that travels with the row. The two agree until the fourth movement of a pair and part company after it, which is why neither is worth showing on its own. Both come from the SAME statement.
